ANSWER KEY AND WORKED EXAMPLES

1.1. The tool-use puzzle
After a dominant parietal stroke, a patient understands the instruction and has adequate strength but cannot correctly pantomime brushing their teeth. They recognise the toothbrush, explain its use and can organise a familiar multistep sequence. Which pairing fits?
Answer: B. Ideomotor apraxia; dominant parietofrontal praxis network
Apraxia concerns learned purposeful actions that cannot be explained simply by weakness, sensory loss or misunderstanding. Praxis relies on a distributed network.
Worked example / practice: Ask for a familiar gesture, then check comprehension and strength. Those control checks matter before interpreting a gesture error.

1.2. Four clues together
A patient with a left angular-gyrus-region infarct has difficulty calculating, writing, identifying fingers and distinguishing left from right. Which pairing is most likely?
Answer: C. Gerstmann syndrome; dominant inferior parietal region
The classic Gerstmann cluster is acalculia, agraphia, finger agnosia and left-right disorientation. The full cluster is uncommon and localisation is not perfectly specific.
Worked example / practice: Mnemonic practice: name the four features without looking, then link them to the dominant inferior parietal region.

1.3. A changed social filter
After orbitofrontal contusions in a road collision, a previously considerate person makes tactless remarks and risky choices despite intact basic language. Which pairing fits best?
Answer: B. Disinhibition; orbitofrontal and ventromedial prefrontal networks
Orbitofrontal and ventromedial networks contribute to evaluating outcomes and regulating social behaviour. Injury can change judgement without producing a major language deficit.
Worked example / practice: Compare this with a planning problem: poor social restraint and poor sequencing can reflect different, overlapping frontal systems.

2.1. One specific trigger
DSM-5-TR. For two years, an adult has immediate intense fear around dogs, avoids streets where dogs may appear and cannot make routine visits. The fear is disproportionate and not about social evaluation or escape from panic. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: A. Specific phobia
Specific phobia involves persistent disproportionate fear tied to a particular object or situation, with immediate anxiety, avoidance or distressed endurance and meaningful impairment. The trigger and functional impact matter.
Worked example / practice: Disliking an animal differs from a fear pattern that repeatedly prevents necessary activities.

2.2. Distance from an attachment figure
DSM-5-TR. For eight months, an adult has excessive fear of separation from their partner, persistent worries that the partner will be harmed, reluctance to leave home and physical distress when apart, seriously limiting work despite no current danger. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: A. Separation anxiety disorder
Adult separation anxiety disorder involves developmentally inappropriate excessive separation fears, associated symptoms and impairment, usually persisting at least six months. It is not diagnosed from affection or missing someone alone.
Worked example / practice: Ask whether separation itself is the feared event, rather than transport, social scrutiny or a realistic current danger.

2.3. Speech changes with the setting
DSM-5-TR. A child speaks freely at home but consistently cannot speak at school for three months beyond the initial settling-in period. They know the school language, and the pattern interferes with learning and friendships. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: D. Selective mutism
Selective mutism involves a consistent failure to speak in specific expected settings despite speech elsewhere, with interference and sufficient duration. Lack of language knowledge and other better explanations must be excluded.
Worked example / practice: Check language familiarity, hearing, developmental history and setting differences before interpreting silence as a disorder.

3.1. Thalamic relay
A tracer study follows a sensory pathway to its initial cortical destination before any first-order thalamic relay. Which modality most clearly fits this exception to the usual introductory sensory-relay pattern?
Answer: C. Olfaction
Initial olfactory cortical processing is unusual in bypassing a first-order thalamic relay. Later olfactory processing can still involve thalamic circuits.
Worked example / practice: Vision uses the lateral geniculate nucleus on a major route to primary visual cortex; olfaction follows a different initial route.

3.2. Keeping time inside
A lesion disrupts light-based synchronisation of the central circadian pacemaker while leaving basic retinal detection intact. Which hypothalamic target is the most direct candidate?
Answer: B. Suprachiasmatic nucleus
The suprachiasmatic nucleus helps coordinate circadian rhythms and receives information related to environmental light.
Worked example / practice: A regular light-dark cycle can help synchronise the clock. The clock is more than simply a conscious decision to feel sleepy.

3.3. Myelin's contribution
Two otherwise comparable axons differ in myelination. In the myelinated axon, active spike regeneration is concentrated at nodes rather than distributed continuously along each internode. Which account explains faster conduction?
Answer: B. It supports rapid saltatory conduction between nodes
Myelin alters electrical properties of the axon and supports regeneration of action potentials at nodes of Ranvier.
Worked example / practice: Picture the nodes as sites where the signal is actively regenerated, not as literal gaps that the signal magically jumps across.

4.1. Socratic curiosity
What is guided discovery in CBT?
Answer: C. Using collaborative questions to help someone examine meanings and alternatives
Guided discovery supports exploration rather than a debate the therapist must win. Questions should be responsive to the person's experience.
Worked example / practice: 'What would you say to a friend in the same position?' can help someone generate a different perspective.

4.2. The all-or-nothing trap
Which statement is an example of all-or-nothing thinking?
Answer: A. If I do not get full marks, the whole effort was worthless
All-or-nothing thinking reduces a continuum to two extremes. Identifying it can open up more nuanced evaluation.
Worked example / practice: A score of 12 out of 15 includes successful learning and areas to practise; it does not need to be labelled total success or total failure.

4.3. Exposure's purpose
Which is the best general description of therapeutic exposure?
Answer: A. Planned contact with feared but sufficiently safe situations to support new learning
Exposure is collaborative and tailored to a formulation. Learning can occur even when some anxiety remains; safety and consent matter.
Worked example / practice: In a fictional graded plan, someone might begin by looking at a lift before later practising a short ride with agreed support.

5.1. An irregular verb in the right frame
Complete the past question matching the reply: A: An ___ tú Máire inné? B: Chonaic, ag an stáisiún.
Answer: A. bhfaca
Some irregular verbs have different independent and dependent past forms. Chonaic contrasts with an bhfaca; chuaigh contrasts with an ndeachaigh. The question particle is part of the grammatical context.
Worked example / practice: Chonaic mé í, but an bhfaca tú í? = I saw her, but did you see her?

5.2. Choosing a place to live
Read: Tá an chéad árasán níos saoire, ach tá sé i bhfad ón obair. Tá an dara ceann gar don oifig, agus sin an ceann a roghnaigh mé. What determined the choice?
Answer: C. The second flat’s proximity to work
Compare the reasons given for a housing decision rather than choosing the first attractive feature. Cé go introduces a concession: although something is true, another factor determines the decision.
Worked example / practice: Cé go bhfuil sé níos mó, tá sé rófhada ón obair = although it is bigger, it is too far from work.

5.3. Possession changes hands
Read: Bhí na heochracha againne, ach thugamar do na comharsana iad ar maidin. Tá siad acu anois. Who has the keys now?
Answer: B. The neighbours
Againn, agaibh and acu express possession by us, you plural and them. Follow any transfer or contrast in the message rather than selecting the first person mentioned.
Worked example / practice: Bhí na ticéid againn, ach tá siad agaibh anois = We had the tickets, but you plural have them now.

6.1. The bass staff
In bass clef, what are the staff lines from bottom to top?
Answer: C. G B D F A
The bass clef marks F on the fourth line from the bottom. Its lines are G B D F A.
Worked example / practice: The spaces are A C E G. Draw five lines and label the fourth line F before filling the rest.

6.2. A minor triad
Which notes form an A minor triad?
Answer: A. A C E
A minor triad contains a minor third above the root and a perfect fifth. A-C is the minor third.
Worked example / practice: Compare A C E with A C-sharp E. Changing only the third changes the chord quality from minor to major.

6.3. First inversion
Which voicing is a C major triad in first inversion, with the lowest note written first?
Answer: A. E G C
In first inversion, the third of the chord is in the bass. The chord remains C major even though E is lowest.
Worked example / practice: Root position: C E G. First inversion: E G C. Second inversion: G C E.
