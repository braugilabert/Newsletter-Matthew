ANSWER KEY AND WORKED EXAMPLES

1.1. Words that will not come
After a left middle cerebral artery stroke affecting the inferior frontal language network, a right-handed teacher speaks in short, effortful phrases. Simple comprehension is relatively preserved, and the right arm is weak. Which syndrome and network fit best?
Answer: C. Broca-type aphasia; dominant frontal language network
Effortful, agrammatic language with relatively preserved simple comprehension suggests a nonfluent aphasia. Broca-type presentations often involve a wider frontal network than one small cortical area.
Worked example / practice: Compare: fluent speech with poor comprehension points towards a posterior language-network syndrome. Try describing the contrast in one sentence.

1.2. Fluent but hard to follow
Following a left posterior temporal stroke, a patient speaks easily but uses invented words and does not understand simple spoken instructions. Hearing is intact. Which pairing is most likely?
Answer: A. Wernicke-type aphasia; dominant posterior temporal language network
Fluency does not guarantee meaningful language. Impaired comprehension plus fluent, disorganised speech fits a Wernicke-type pattern, with variable involvement of nearby language regions.
Worked example / practice: A person says a long sentence but cannot follow 'close your eyes': test language comprehension rather than assuming a hearing problem.

1.3. Half a plate
After a right parietal stroke, a patient eats food only from the right half of the plate and ignores people on the left. Basic visual fields are intact on examination. Which pairing fits?
Answer: C. Left hemispatial neglect; right frontoparietal attention network
Neglect is impaired attention to contralesional space, not simply loss of vision. Right parietal and connected attention networks are commonly involved.
Worked example / practice: A clock drawing with all numbers crowded onto the right can suggest neglect. Contrast it with a visual-field defect, which needs separate testing.

2.1. Three weeks unlike the usual self
DSM-5-TR. Medical and substance-related explanations have been assessed and excluded. For three weeks, an adult has depressed mood, no enjoyment, early waking, low energy, poor concentration and marked guilt nearly every day, and is missing work. There is no history of mania or hypomania. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: C. Major depressive disorder
A major depressive episode involves sustained depressed mood and/or loss of interest with a sufficient accompanying symptom pattern, impairment and relevant exclusions. A history of mania or hypomania changes the diagnostic differential.
Worked example / practice: Distinguish a sustained impairing syndrome from a single sad day. A vignette supports a best-fit answer, not a complete clinical assessment.

2.2. A low mood across years
DSM-5-TR. Medical and substance-related explanations have been excluded. An adult has felt low most days for three years, with poor self-esteem, fatigue and hopelessness. There have been no symptom-free intervals longer than a month and no elevated mood episodes. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: B. Persistent depressive disorder
Persistent depressive disorder in adults involves depressed mood over at least two years with accompanying chronic symptoms and no extended symptom-free gaps. Chronicity distinguishes it from an isolated short episode.
Worked example / practice: Construct a timeline: years of symptoms and length of symptom-free intervals can matter as much as symptom intensity.

2.3. A sustained and disruptive high
DSM-5-TR. For nine days, an adult has unusually elevated mood and increased energy, sleeps two hours without tiredness, speaks rapidly, shows racing thoughts and grandiose plans, and makes disastrous purchases requiring hospital care. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?
Answer: C. Bipolar I disorder
A manic episode includes a distinct sustained mood and energy change with associated symptoms and marked impairment, hospitalisation or psychosis. One manic episode can establish bipolar I when other explanations are excluded.
Worked example / practice: Reduced need for sleep means sleeping little without feeling tired, rather than wanting sleep but being unable to obtain it.

3.1. Dopamine beyond pleasure
Which description best captures dopamine's roles in the brain?
Answer: C. Movement, motivation, learning and reward-related signalling
Dopamine has several pathways and receptor types. Its functions cannot be reduced to a single emotion.
Worked example / practice: A reward arriving unexpectedly can change dopamine-cell firing in some circuits. Separately, nigrostriatal dopamine supports movement.

3.2. Serotonin without the slogan
Which statement is most accurate about serotonin?
Answer: C. It contributes to several processes, including mood, sleep and appetite
Serotonin acts through multiple receptor types in different circuits. A simple 'more serotonin means more happiness' rule is misleading.
Worked example / practice: Try listing three systems that use serotonin. Mood regulation, sleep-related processes and appetite provide a starting set.

3.3. Oxytocin in context
Which statement about oxytocin is most accurate?
Answer: B. It has reproductive roles and context-dependent effects on social behaviour
Oxytocin participates in childbirth and milk ejection and influences some social processes. Social effects depend on the person, task and context.
Worked example / practice: Replace the label 'love hormone' with a fuller sentence: reproductive physiology plus context-sensitive social signalling.

4.1. CBT's central link
Which relationship is central to a standard CBT formulation?
Answer: B. Thoughts, emotions and behaviours influence one another in context
CBT examines patterns that maintain distress and tests more helpful alternatives. The aim is balanced and workable thinking, not forced positivity.
Worked example / practice: Situation: no reply to a message. Thought: 'They dislike me.' Alternative: 'They may be busy.' Behaviour: wait rather than send repeated messages.

4.2. Where cognitive therapy began
Who is particularly associated with developing cognitive therapy in the 1960s?
Answer: A. Aaron T. Beck
Beck developed cognitive therapy, initially through work on depression. Modern CBT also draws on behavioural traditions and other contributors.
Worked example / practice: Keep the historical pairings distinct: Beck -> cognitive therapy; Linehan -> DBT; Freud -> psychoanalysis.

4.3. DBT's original developer
Who developed dialectical behaviour therapy with colleagues?
Answer: B. Marsha Linehan
DBT grew from behavioural work with people experiencing severe emotion dysregulation and suicidal behaviour, combining acceptance and change strategies.
Worked example / practice: Remember the balance with a fictional phrase: 'Your feelings make sense, and we can practise a different response.'

5.1. How are you?
Choose the ordinary Irish question meaning 'How are you?'
Answer: C. Conas atá tú?
Conas asks how. Cá asks where. Cé asks who. Conas atá tú? is a common standard form, with regional alternatives in everyday speech.
Worked example / practice: Mini-dialogue: Conas atá tú? Tá mé go maith, go raibh maith agat. Meaning: How are you? I am well, thank you.

5.2. I have a book
Complete: Tá leabhar ___. Meaning: I have a book.
Answer: C. agam
Irish commonly expresses possession using tá plus ag. Agam means at me; tá leabhar agam literally frames a book as being at me.
Worked example / practice: Try the same pattern: Tá peann agam. Meaning: I have a pen. Tá cat agam means I have a cat.

5.3. I like music
Choose the sentence meaning 'I like music.'
Answer: B. Is maith liom ceol.
Is maith liom is a common way to express liking something. Liom is the combined form of le plus mé.
Worked example / practice: Pattern swap: Is maith liom tae = I like tea. Is maith liom an leabhar = I like the book.

6.1. One beat at a time
In 4/4, with the crotchet (quarter note) as one beat, how long is a minim (half note)?
Answer: A. Two beats
A minim lasts twice as long as a crotchet. The beat unit matters when translating note values into beats.
Worked example / practice: Clap: crotchet, crotchet, minim. Count 1, 2, 3-4. The pattern fills one 4/4 bar.

6.2. The major-scale recipe
Which interval pattern builds a major scale from its tonic?
Answer: A. Tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone
The major pattern is W-W-H-W-W-W-H, where W is a whole tone and H is a semitone.
Worked example / practice: C major: C D E F G A B C. Check the semitones at E-F and B-C.

6.3. Build a C major triad
Which notes form a root-position C major triad?
Answer: B. C E G
A major triad contains a root, a major third and a perfect fifth. C-E is a major third; C-G is a perfect fifth.
Worked example / practice: Play or sing C, E, G separately, then together if possible. Lower E to E-flat to hear the change to minor.
