KEY 01

Neuropsychology case files

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

1.1 · The repetition gap

A patient with a small dominant temporoparietal stroke speaks fairly fluently and understands conversation, but cannot accurately repeat even short unfamiliar phrases. Which pairing is most likely?

Answer: A. Conduction aphasia; dorsal language pathways

Disproportionately impaired repetition with relatively preserved fluency and comprehension suggests conduction aphasia. Dorsal perisylvian connections and cortical regions can be involved.

Worked example / practice: Build a three-column language checklist: fluency, comprehension and repetition. Conduction aphasia has its biggest weakness in the third column.

1.2 · An island of repetition

Following a left frontal watershed infarct that spares the core perisylvian language network, spontaneous speech is sparse, comprehension is relatively good, and repetition is preserved. Which pairing fits best?

Answer: C. Transcortical motor aphasia; frontal regions around the language network

Preserved repetition distinguishes this nonfluent pattern from many Broca-type presentations. Lesions can isolate otherwise functioning language circuitry from initiation systems.

Worked example / practice: Compare two nonfluent speakers: if repetition is strikingly good in one, consider a transcortical pattern in the fictional case.

1.3 · More than word finding

A large left middle cerebral artery infarct causes very limited speech, poor comprehension and poor repetition. Right-sided weakness is also present. Which pairing is most likely?

Answer: C. Global aphasia; extensive dominant perisylvian network injury

Global aphasia affects expression and comprehension across several language tasks, usually reflecting widespread dominant language-network injury.

Worked example / practice: Compare: naming difficulty alone with otherwise fluent, comprehensible speech would be a much milder anomic pattern.

KEY 02

Mental health case files

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

2.1 · A history beyond the lows

DSM-5-TR. An adult has a documented major depressive episode and a previous five-day period of elevated mood, increased energy, little need for sleep, more talking, racing thoughts and increased projects. The change was clear but caused no marked impairment, psychosis or hospitalisation. There has never been mania. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: B. Bipolar II disorder

Bipolar II disorder requires hypomanic and major depressive episodes without a manic episode. Hypomania is an observable sustained change without the marked impairment, hospitalisation or psychosis that would make the elevated episode manic.

Worked example / practice: Always ask about past elevated periods when evaluating a depressive presentation; current low mood alone does not establish unipolar depression.

2.2 · Fluctuations below episode thresholds

DSM-5-TR. Medical and substance-related explanations are excluded. For three years, an adult has frequent periods of mildly elevated energy and periods of depressive symptoms, present for more than half the time without gaps longer than two months. No period has met full hypomanic, manic or major depressive episode criteria, but functioning suffers. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: D. Cyclothymic disorder

Cyclothymic disorder in adults involves at least two years of numerous subthreshold hypomanic and depressive symptoms, present for much of the time, without long symptom-free intervals or a history meeting full mood-episode criteria.

Worked example / practice: Subthreshold does not mean unimportant: impairment may occur even when no single period meets a full episode definition.

2.3 · Worry that spreads across life

DSM-5-TR. Medical and substance-related explanations are excluded. For eight months, an adult worries excessively on most days about money, health and ordinary work tasks, struggles to control it, and has restlessness, muscle tension, fatigue and poor sleep with impairment. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: D. Generalized anxiety disorder

Generalized anxiety disorder involves difficult-to-control excessive worry across several areas with associated symptoms and impairment. DSM-5-TR adult duration is at least six months; it is not restricted to one object or social scrutiny.

Worked example / practice: Ask what the worry concerns, how controllable it feels and its duration, rather than treating worry as diagnostic by itself.

KEY 03

Neuroscience

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

3.1 · The inhibitory workhorse

Which neurotransmitter mediates much of the fast inhibitory signalling in the adult brain?

Answer: A. GABA

GABA is a major inhibitory transmitter in the mature central nervous system. Effects depend on receptor type and ionic conditions.

Worked example / practice: Excitation and inhibition work together: inhibiting one neuron can even disinhibit a downstream neuron.

3.2 · The excitatory workhorse

Which transmitter mediates much of the fast excitatory signalling in the central nervous system?

Answer: A. Glutamate

Glutamate acts at several receptor families, including AMPA and NMDA receptors, and is central to excitatory transmission.

Worked example / practice: AMPA and NMDA are receptor names, while glutamate is a transmitter that can activate them.

3.3 · Acetylcholine has more than one job

Which pairing is most accurate?

Answer: C. Neuromuscular transmission and contributions to attention and memory

Acetylcholine acts at skeletal neuromuscular junctions and in central circuits involved in cognition and arousal.

Worked example / practice: The same transmitter can work in the peripheral nervous system and in brain circuits; location changes its role.

KEY 04

Psychotherapy toolkit

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

4.1 · The dialectic

Which statement best expresses the acceptance-change balance in DBT?

Answer: B. I can accept my current experience while working to change harmful patterns

Dialectical thinking makes room for apparently opposing truths. Validation does not mean endorsing every behaviour.

Worked example / practice: A therapist can validate disappointment while helping a client choose a more effective way to communicate it.

4.2 · The four skills groups

Which list gives the standard DBT skills modules?

Answer: C. Mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness

These four modules teach practical skills, while comprehensive DBT includes additional treatment components beyond a skills class.

Worked example / practice: Sort an example: noticing an urge without immediately acting fits mindfulness; making a clear request fits interpersonal effectiveness.

4.3 · An automatic thought

In CBT, what is an automatic thought?

Answer: C. A quickly arising interpretation of a situation

Automatic thoughts can be words or images and are often taken for granted. Identifying one allows it to be examined.

Worked example / practice: 'I stumbled over a word, so everyone thinks I am incompetent' is an interpretation to examine, not a conclusion to accept automatically.

KEY 05

A little Gaeilge

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

5.1 · The negative of tá

Complete: ___ mé tuirseach. Meaning: I am not tired.

Answer: B. Níl

The present negative form of bí is níl. A simple ní before tá is not the standard construction.

Worked example / practice: Positive: Tá mé tuirseach. Negative: Níl mé tuirseach. Meaning: I am tired / I am not tired.

5.2 · Where is the bus?

Which question means 'Where is the bus?'

Answer: C. Cá bhfuil an bus?

Cá bhfuil is a common where-is construction. An bus means the bus.

Worked example / practice: Useful travel question: Cá bhfuil an stáisiún? Meaning: Where is the station?

5.3 · Saying your name

Which sentence means 'My name is Matthew'?

Answer: C. Matthew is ainm dom.

X is ainm dom is a standard naming pattern. Dom is the combined form of do plus mé.

Worked example / practice: Swap the name: Aoife is ainm dom. To ask someone's name: Cad is ainm duit?

KEY 06

Music theory, finally

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

6.1 · Quavers in a bar

How many quavers (eighth notes) fill a 4/4 bar containing only quavers?

Answer: B. Eight

Each quaver lasts half a crotchet beat. Four crotchet beats therefore contain eight quavers.

Worked example / practice: Count 1-and-2-and-3-and-4-and while tapping once on each spoken syllable.

6.2 · A dot changes duration

In 4/4, how many crotchet beats does a dotted minim (dotted half note) last?

Answer: C. Three

A dot adds half of the original note's duration. A two-beat minim gains one beat, making three.

Worked example / practice: A dotted minim plus one crotchet fills a 4/4 bar: 3 + 1 = 4 beats.

6.3 · Sharps and flats

In standard Western notation, what does a sharp normally do to a note?

Answer: B. Raises it by one semitone

A sharp changes pitch, not loudness. Its precise frequency depends on the tuning system, but the notated interval is one semitone.

Worked example / practice: On a piano, C-sharp is the next key above C. Volume instructions use other markings such as forte or piano.
