KEY 01

Neuropsychology case files

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

1.1 · New days that disappear

After bilateral medial temporal injury from encephalitis, a patient repeatedly forgets conversations held minutes earlier, although digit span and old motor skills remain relatively good. Which pairing fits?

Answer: C. Anterograde declarative amnesia; hippocampal memory systems

Bilateral medial temporal damage can severely disrupt new declarative learning while sparing aspects of immediate memory and procedural learning.

Worked example / practice: Remembering how to ride a bicycle but forgetting meeting the instructor illustrates different memory systems.

1.2 · A familiar face becomes unfamiliar

After a right occipitotemporal infarct, a patient cannot recognise familiar people by face, but identifies them by voice and can describe facial features. Which pairing fits best?

Answer: A. Prosopagnosia; ventral occipitotemporal face-processing network

A selective face-recognition difficulty despite adequate basic vision suggests prosopagnosia. Right-sided or bilateral ventral visual-network damage may be involved.

Worked example / practice: Recognising a friend by their voice while failing to recognise their face helps distinguish a face-processing problem from a general memory problem.

1.3 · Seen but not recognised

Following bilateral ventral occipitotemporal injury, a patient can copy an object drawing but cannot identify the pictured object. Recognition by touch is intact. They also fail to match a visually presented object to its function without naming it. Which pairing is most likely?

Answer: A. Associative visual agnosia; ventral visual recognition pathways

Preserved copying suggests enough perceptual processing to form a representation, while access to object meaning from vision is impaired. Naming and semantic abilities must also be assessed.

Worked example / practice: If the same object can be recognised by touch, the difficulty is more specific to visual recognition than to loss of all object knowledge.

KEY 02

Mental health case files

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

2.1 · Fear of the next unexpected episode

DSM-5-TR. An adult has recurrent unexpected surges of intense fear with palpitations, shaking, breathlessness and dizziness, peaking within minutes. For two months they have worried persistently about another attack and changed daily activities to avoid one. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: B. Panic disorder

Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus at least a month of persistent concern or maladaptive behaviour change. A panic attack alone is not the same as panic disorder.

Worked example / practice: Distinguish unexpected attacks from fear occurring only during a specific phobic or social situation.

2.2 · When escape feels difficult

DSM-5-TR. For nine months, an adult avoids buses and crowded queues because escape or help might be difficult if they become dizzy or panicky. They need a companion, the fear is disproportionate and life is substantially restricted. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: B. Agoraphobia

Agoraphobia concerns situations where escape or help may feel difficult if incapacitating or embarrassing symptoms occur. DSM-5-TR requires fear across at least two specified situation types and a persistent impairing pattern.

Worked example / practice: A person may fear buses and queues because help feels unavailable, rather than fearing strangers judging their conversational skill.

2.3 · The feared judgement

DSM-5-TR. For a year, an adult fears conversations and meetings because others may notice trembling and judge them negatively. These settings consistently provoke anxiety, are avoided or endured with distress, and disrupt work. Which diagnosis best fits this fictional case?

Answer: A. Social anxiety disorder

Social anxiety disorder centres on possible negative evaluation in social or performance situations, with persistent disproportionate fear, avoidance or distressed endurance and impairment. Ordinary shyness alone is insufficient.

Worked example / practice: Compare fear of being judged while speaking with fear of being unable to escape a building.

KEY 03

Neuroscience

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

3.1 · Alertness and noradrenaline

A manipulation reduces widespread forebrain noradrenaline signalling and alters task-dependent arousal. Which nucleus is the most direct candidate source, rather than a serotonergic, dopaminergic or cholinergic source?

Answer: A. Locus coeruleus

The locus coeruleus projects widely and contributes to arousal, attention and responses to salient events.

Worked example / practice: Use a pairing card: locus coeruleus -> noradrenaline. Raphe nuclei -> serotonin is another useful pairing.

3.2 · Improvement without remembering practice

A patient with bilateral hippocampal injury improves across repeated motor-skill trials but cannot remember the practice sessions. Which additional deficit most closely fits this dissociation?

Answer: B. Poor acquisition of new episodic relations despite improved motor performance

New episodic and relational learning can be severely impaired while some procedural learning survives. The finding supports partly distinct memory systems, not an absence of all learning.

Worked example / practice: Compare recognising the instructor and remembering meeting them yesterday with improving at the repeated movement.

3.3 · Amygdala beyond fear

A region responds during learning about both threatening and rewarding cues, with its response affected by biological significance. Which description best avoids treating that region as a detector of fear alone?

Answer: B. Learning about biologically significant cues and contributing to emotional responses

Amygdala circuits contribute to emotional learning and salience, including but not limited to threat processing.

Worked example / practice: A previously neutral sound can acquire significance through learning. Several connected structures contribute to the response.

KEY 04

Psychotherapy toolkit

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

4.1 · Testing a prediction

A client predicts that asking a question will lead to ridicule. With a therapist, they plan a manageable test and compare the result with the prediction. What is this?

Answer: B. A behavioural experiment

A behavioural experiment gathers evidence about a specific prediction. It is more than simply telling the client to stop worrying.

Worked example / practice: Prediction: 'Everyone will laugh.' Observable outcome: how people actually respond to one ordinary question.

4.2 · Getting moving again

Which example best illustrates behavioural activation?

Answer: B. Scheduling a small meaningful activity and reviewing its effect

Behavioural activation addresses withdrawal and reduced reinforcement through planned engagement. Activities are selected collaboratively and adjusted to circumstances.

Worked example / practice: A small plan might be a ten-minute walk on Tuesday, followed by a brief note about mood and sense of achievement.

4.3 · Looking for evidence

Which question best represents collaborative cognitive reappraisal?

Answer: B. What evidence supports this thought, and what evidence complicates it?

Cognitive reappraisal examines accuracy and usefulness. It does not assume distress is irrational or that real difficulties can be wished away.

Worked example / practice: 'I failed one exam' is specific evidence. 'I will fail everything forever' is a much broader prediction.

KEY 05

A little Gaeilge

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

5.1 · An ordinary greeting

What is the usual reply to Dia duit when greeting one person?

Answer: B. Dia is Muire duit.

Dia is Muire duit is the traditional response to Dia duit. Other greetings and local forms are also used.

Worked example / practice: Two-person dialogue: A: Dia duit. B: Dia is Muire duit. A simple friendly greeting exchange.

5.2 · Counting objects simply

Which standalone number is 'three'?

Answer: A. a trí

A trí is three in the basic counting series. Counting people and counting objects introduce additional patterns.

Worked example / practice: Count aloud: a haon, a dó, a trí, a ceathair, a cúig. This is practice with the abstract counting series.

5.3 · Tea, please

Choose the polite phrase meaning 'a cup of tea, please'.

Answer: A. Cupán tae, le do thoil.

Le do thoil means please when speaking to one person. Le bhur dtoil is the plural form.

Worked example / practice: At a café: Ba mhaith liom cupán tae, le do thoil. Meaning: I would like a cup of tea, please.

KEY 06

Music theory, finally

Answers, explanations and one more small step.

6.1 · The smallest keyboard step

How many semitones separate adjacent keys on a conventionally tuned piano, including black keys?

Answer: B. One

A semitone is the smallest adjacent-key step in the usual twelve-note equal-tempered keyboard system.

Worked example / practice: E to F is one semitone even though both are white keys. C to D is two semitones.

6.2 · An octave

How many semitones are in a standard Western octave?

Answer: B. Twelve

An octave spans twelve semitones in the twelve-note system, while its diatonic letter-name span counts eight notes inclusively.

Worked example / practice: C to the next higher C is an octave. The letter names C D E F G A B C count eight positions including both endpoints.

6.3 · The treble staff

In treble clef, what are the staff lines from bottom to top?

Answer: A. E G B D F

The treble clef identifies the G line, which is the second line from the bottom. The five lines are E G B D F.

Worked example / practice: The spaces from bottom to top spell F A C E. Compare lines and spaces rather than memorising them as one mixed list.
